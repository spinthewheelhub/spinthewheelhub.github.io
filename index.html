How the wheel works

Every line in the entry box becomes one slice of the wheel, and every slice is exactly the same size. When you press SPIN, the wheel is given a strong random push — several full turns plus a random finishing angle — and then slows down gradually, the way a real fairground wheel loses speed, until the pointer settles on a single slice. That slice is your result. Because the final resting angle is chosen by a random number generator before the animation even starts, the pretty spinning is just the reveal: the outcome is decided by chance, not by the animation, the order of your list, or how long you watch it turn.

Using it takes three steps. First, type or paste your entries, one per line — names for a raffle, options for a decision, students for a classroom question. Second, if you like, shuffle the list so the slices land in a mixed order; shuffling changes nothing about the odds, but it looks fairer to an audience, which matters more than people admit. Third, spin, announce the winner, and either spin again with the same list or remove the winner and spin for the next prize. Our full walkthrough on how to run a fair giveaway with a picker wheel covers the whole flow, from collecting entries to announcing results people trust.

The wheel handles anywhere from two entries to a few hundred. With very long lists the labels get small, so for a big raffle we suggest spinning in rounds — or pasting the list in batches — rather than squinting at a wheel with three hundred hair-thin slices. Two entries is the minimum: with a single entry there is nothing for chance to decide.

Is it actually fair? How the randomness works

Fairness on a picker wheel comes down to two things: equal slices and an unpredictable stopping point. SpinTheWheelHub gives every entry an identical slice of the circle, so each entry's chance of winning is exactly one divided by the number of entries — with ten names, everyone has a 10% chance, no more and no less. The stopping point comes from your browser's built-in random number generator, the same technology used for shuffling music playlists and dealing digital card games. It does not remember previous spins, it has no favourites, and it cannot be nudged by where a name sits in your list.

One honest caveat, because fairness deserves honesty: a browser wheel is perfect for giveaways, classrooms, games and everyday decisions, but it is not a certified gambling device and should not be used where money, legal prizes or regulated draws are involved. For everything else — the vast majority of spins — it is as fair as drawing names from a hat, and considerably harder to argue with. There is also a human side to fairness. A wheel is transparent in a way a hat is not: everyone can see every name on the wheel before the spin, watch it slow down, and see exactly where the pointer lands. Nobody has to trust that the person drawing the slip didn't peek, fold one corner, or practise a reach. How to Run a Fair Giveaway with a Picker Wheel

A complete, practical playbook — from writing the rules to announcing a winner everyone believes in.

A giveaway lives or dies on trust. The prize can be modest, the audience small, the whole thing over in an afternoon — but if even one entrant walks away suspecting the draw was rigged, the giveaway has done the opposite of its job. A picker wheel is the easiest way to make a draw visibly fair, because the audience watches every name go on the wheel and watches the pointer land. This guide walks through the whole process, step by step, the way experienced hosts actually run it.

Step 1: Write the rules before you collect a single entry

Most giveaway disputes are not about the spin at all. They are about questions nobody answered in advance. Who can enter? Does commenting twice count twice? When does entry close? What happens if the winner never replies? Decide all of it up front and publish it with the giveaway announcement. Keep the rules short enough that people actually read them: eligibility, how to enter, the entry deadline, the draw date, how the winner will be contacted, and how long they have to respond before you redraw. If your prize has real monetary value or your region regulates prize promotions, check the local rules too — this guide covers fairness and good practice, not legal advice.

One decision matters more than the rest: whether one person can hold more than one entry. Both approaches are fair, but they are fair in different ways. One entry per person gives everyone identical odds. Multiple entries — say, one per comment or per tag — rewards engagement but lets an enthusiastic entrant outweigh a quiet one. Either is defensible; switching between them after entries open is not. State your choice in the rules and stick to it.

Step 2: Collect and clean the entries

When entry closes, gather the names into a plain list — a spreadsheet column or a text file is perfect. Then clean it. Remove obvious bot accounts and entries that broke your published rules. If your rules say one entry per person, remove duplicates now; if they allow multiples, leave them in. Read the list once for typos, because a misspelled winner name creates a pointless argument later. This cleaned list is exactly what you will paste into the picker wheel on our homepage, one name per line.

If the draw will be public, consider sharing the final entry list before the spin — a screenshot or a post listing everyone who made it in. It takes two minutes and it closes off the most common complaint in all of giveaway-land: "my name was never even entered." People who can find their own name on the list go into the draw calm, and calm audiences believe results.

Step 3: Set up the wheel in front of your audience

Paste the list into the entry box and check the count shown under it against your cleaned list — the numbers should match exactly. Shuffle the entries before you spin. Shuffling does not change anyone's odds, since every slice is the same size, but it mixes the slices so no one can claim the list was arranged to favour a position. Then share your screen, record the draw, or spin live — whatever your audience can watch. Show the full wheel, not a cropped corner of it. The entire point of using a wheel instead of quietly picking a name yourself is that the audience sees the same thing you see.

Before the real spin, do one practice spin with the sound on and the screen shared, just to confirm everything is visible and working. Then clear any practice result from people's minds by saying so out loud: "That was a test — the real draw starts now." It sounds like a small thing, but clearly separating test spins from the official spin prevents the single most awkward giveaway moment: a test winner thinking they have won.

Step 4: Spin, announce, and screenshot

Press spin and let the wheel do its work. When it stops, announce the winner by the exact name shown, and take a screenshot of the result before you touch anything. That screenshot is your receipt — post it with the announcement so latecomers can see the outcome for themselves. Then contact the winner the way your rules promised: a direct message, an email, a comment tag. Public announcement plus private contact is the belt-and-braces approach; doing only one of the two is where winners get missed.

Drawing several prizes? Announce the order in advance — biggest prize first or last, your choice — and after each winner, use the remove-winner option and spin again. Removing winners matters: it guarantees nobody takes two prizes unless your rules explicitly allow it, and the shrinking wheel makes each subsequent draw more dramatic, not less.

Step 5: Handle the awkward cases before they handle you

The winner doesn't respond. Your rules already set a deadline — 48 hours is common. When it passes, say so publicly, then redraw from the same list with the same wheel. Never quietly hand the prize to a friend of the winner or to the runner-up; a redraw is the only outcome nobody can dispute.

The winner turns out to be ineligible. Perhaps they are under the age limit, or in a country you excluded. Thank them, explain briefly, and redraw. Vague silence breeds conspiracy theories; a short honest explanation ends the story.

Someone accuses you of rigging it. Point to the evidence: the published rules, the shared entry list, the recording or screenshot. This is why you built that trail. A host who can show every step rarely has to argue about any of them.

A tie or a technical glitch. Wheels cannot tie — the pointer lands on one slice. If your screen froze mid-spin, the honest fix is a fresh spin in front of the same audience, announced as such. Everyone prefers a clean re-spin to a guessed result.

A short checklist to keep beside you

  1. Rules published before entries open, including the redraw policy.
  2. Entries closed, cleaned, deduplicated according to the rules.
  3. Entry list shared with the audience.
  4. List pasted into the wheel; entry count matches.
  5. Entries shuffled; one clearly announced test spin.
  6. Official spin done on a shared or recorded screen.
  7. Winner screenshotted, announced publicly, contacted privately.
  8. Winners removed before any further prize spins.
  9. Deadline watched; redraw held publicly if it passes.

Follow that list and your giveaway will be boring in exactly the right way — all the excitement in the spin, none in the aftermath. Spin the Wheel in the Classroom: Teacher Ideas

Fair cold-calling, painless group-making and reward wheels that keep the whole class in play.

Every teacher knows the pattern. You ask a question, and the same four hands go up — the confident, the keen, the ones who did the reading — while the rest of the class studies the grain of their desks. It is not laziness. Most students are running a quiet calculation: volunteering and being wrong feels risky, so the safest move is silence. A picker wheel changes that calculation completely, because being chosen stops being a performance and becomes luck. Nobody judges a student the wheel picked, and — just as usefully — nobody blames the teacher either. The wheel is the fairest colleague you will ever have: it has no favourites, no memory, and no mercy, and students accept its decisions with a shrug they would never give a teacher's choice.

Setup takes two minutes. Type your class list into the wheel on our homepage, one name per line, and keep the tab open. Because the list lives in your browser, you can return to it all day. Here is how teachers put it to work.

Cold-calling without the dread

The classic use: spin to choose who answers the next question. Done well, it keeps the whole class following along, because anyone could be next. Two refinements make it kinder and more effective. First, spin before you ask the question, not after — the chosen student then listens to the question knowing it is theirs, and everyone else relaxes into listening rather than rehearsing. Second, keep a "pass and return" norm: a student may pass once, but their name stays in and the wheel will come back to them later in the lesson. That single rule removes the terror (nobody is trapped) while keeping the accountability (nobody escapes forever). If you want no repeats within a lesson, remove each name after it is picked; if you want everyone to stay alert all lesson, leave names in.

Random groups that end the friendship draft

Group work collapses when students pick their own groups: friends cluster, the same students are left standing, and the lesson starts with a social negotiation instead of learning. The wheel ends it. Spin for the first member of group one, remove the name, spin again, and build each group in front of the class. Because the process is public and mechanical, complaints evaporate — students will argue with a teacher's grouping for ten minutes and accept the wheel's in ten seconds. For paired work, spin twice per pair. For a jigsaw activity, spin to assign topics to groups after the groups are formed, so nobody feels handed the "boring" topic by a human.

The reward wheel

Replace the name list with privileges and you have a reward wheel: choose the class playlist song, five minutes of free reading, pick tomorrow's warm-up game, sit in the good chair, be first in line, choose a classroom job, pick the story, earn a homework pass. Spin it for the class at the end of a good week, or let an individual student who earned it take the spin. Reward wheels work because the prizes cost nothing and the anticipation is the real reward — a wheel full of small delights generates more excitement than one big prize, and it can be spun far more often.

Subject games and lesson mechanics

The wheel is a flexible game engine. Put numbers on it and it becomes a dice substitute for maths practice — spin twice and multiply, add, or find the difference. Put vocabulary words on it and spin to choose which word the class must use in a sentence. Put question types on it — explain, compare, predict, give an example — and spin to decide how the class will engage with a text. In PE and music, spin for the next activity or warm-up. For revision, load the wheel with topic names and let it choose the order, which stops you unconsciously saving the hard topic for a day that never comes.

One more quiet superpower: the wheel as tiebreaker and referee. Which team goes first, which class project gets the wall space, whose question gets answered when time runs out — small decisions that would otherwise eat minutes and goodwill. Spin, accept, move on. The lesson keeps its pace, and the class learns something genuinely useful: that fair processes end arguments better than loud voices do.

Making it work on a real classroom screen

Project the wheel large and spin with the class watching — the shared suspense is half the value. Keep your class list saved as a text file so you can paste it in each morning in seconds, and keep a second list ready with first names only if your school prefers not to display full names on screen. For younger classes, read the result aloud and celebrate it; for older students, deadpan works better — the wheel needs no hype from you. And if a spin lands on a student who is absent, just remove the name and spin again without ceremony; the class will treat it as the ritual it is.

Used daily, the wheel quietly rewires classroom expectations: everyone prepares, because anyone can be chosen; everyone relaxes, because being chosen means nothing about them. That combination — alert and unembarrassed — is exactly the state good learning happens in. Random Picker vs Drawing Names: Which Is Fairer?

Hats, slips of paper and software, examined honestly — where each method quietly bends the odds.

For centuries, the fair way to choose was to write names on slips, drop them in a hat, and draw one out. It feels fair. It looks fair. And in the ways that matter most, it often is not — while the software picker that feels like a shortcut turns out to be the stricter referee. This is not a sales pitch; both methods have real strengths, and there are still occasions where paper wins. But if fairness is the question, the details are worth a closer look, because randomness is a subtle thing and human hands are subtly biased things.

Where the hat quietly goes wrong

Slips are not identical. Fairness in a hat draw depends on every slip being indistinguishable, and they almost never are. One is folded, one is flat. One is a torn corner of card, one is thin paper. The slip written last sits on top; the one written first sinks. A drawer who has handled the slips — folding them, say — has information no one else has, even if they never consciously use it. None of this requires a cheat. It only requires physics and human fingers.

The draw is hard to audit. After a hat draw, what evidence exists? A slip with a name on it. Nobody can verify that every name went in, that none went in twice, or that the drawer's hand didn't drift toward a familiar texture. The audience must trust the process because they cannot see it. Trust is a fine thing among friends and a fragile thing among five hundred giveaway entrants.

Repeats and removals get messy. Drawing three winners without replacement means setting slips aside carefully; with replacement means putting them back and re-mixing convincingly. In practice, busy hosts do both halfway, and the odds drift in ways nobody can reconstruct afterwards.

What a software picker does differently

A picker wheel like the one on our homepage attacks each of those weaknesses directly. Every entry gets a mathematically identical slice of the circle — not approximately identical paper, exactly identical geometry. The winning slice is chosen by the browser's random number generator, which has no hands, no memory of where names sit in the list, and no way to favour the entry typed first or last. Removing a winner is a single click and is total: the name is gone, not resting at the bottom of a hat. And the whole process happens in the open, on a screen the audience can watch, with every name visible before the spin. The audit trail is the show itself.

Software has its own honest limitations, and fairness demands naming them. A browser picker is not independently certified the way a regulated lottery machine is; you are trusting well-tested, standard random number generation rather than an audited device. It also produces no physical artefact — some ceremonies want a slip of paper to hold up. And on a screen nobody watches, software is worse than a hat, because a dishonest host can simply spin in private until they like the result. The fairness of a picker lives in its visibility: spin once, in public, and it is the fairest tool most of us will ever use.

So which should you choose?

For a family choosing who washes up, honestly, either — the stakes are a sponge. For a classroom, the wheel wins on repeatability and on keeping the teacher above suspicion. For a giveaway with an audience that does not know you, the wheel wins decisively, because strangers cannot inspect your hat but they can watch your screen; our giveaway playbook is built around exactly that visibility. For a wedding raffle or a village fete where the charm is the ritual — the drumroll, the child's hand in the hat — paper has a theatre software cannot match, and the tiny biases of folded slips are a fair price for the moment. Fairness, in the end, is partly mathematics and partly belief. The wheel is better mathematics, presented in a way that earns belief. The hat is decent mathematics wrapped in better theatre. Choose with your eyes open, and if the result matters to anyone beyond the room, choose the method your audience can watch.

How to Pick a Winner on Instagram or YouTube Live

Collect entries, verify them, share your screen and spin — a live draw your audience will believe.

Picking a giveaway winner live is the gold standard of social media draws, because it replaces the sentence every audience distrusts — "congratulations to our winner!" appearing from nowhere — with a moment they watched happen. A live spin cannot be edited, re-rolled or quietly arranged, and viewers know it. The catch is that live also means live: no retakes, chat scrolling past, and a winner who may or may not be watching. This guide covers the full run — preparation, the broadcast itself, and the follow-up — so the draw feels like a celebration instead of a technical incident.

Before you go live: entries and verification

Close entries at the time your post promised, then export or copy the entrants into a list. On Instagram that usually means working through the comments on the giveaway post; on YouTube, the comments under the video. Check each entry against your stated rules — did they follow, tag, subscribe, comment the required word, whatever you asked — and remove the ones that did not, plus duplicate entries if your rules allow only one per person. Do this before the stream, not during it; nothing kills a live draw like watching the host scroll through comments doing admin. What you want at showtime is a finished list, ready to paste into the picker wheel. Our fair giveaway guide covers rule-writing and cleaning in more depth.

Prepare your broadcast scene in advance. On YouTube Live, set up a screen-share source showing the browser with the wheel loaded and the entry list pasted in, and do a private test stream to check the wheel is legible at stream resolution — small text on your monitor can be mush on a phone. On Instagram Live, you cannot share a desktop screen directly, so creators either point the camera at a screen (workable, but keep it steady and close) or run the stream from a setup where the wheel fills the frame. Test the framing beforehand; a wobbling, half-visible wheel undermines the very trust the live draw exists to create.

During the live: run the draw like a tiny show

Open by restating the essentials in thirty seconds: what the prize is, how people entered, how many entries there are, and what happens if the winner does not claim it. Show the entry list on the wheel and scroll or point out that names are there — invite viewers to spot their own name; the chat lighting up with "I see me!" is the sound of trust being built. Shuffle the entries on camera. Then, clearly: "This is the official spin." One spin, no practice rolls mixed in — if you want a practice spin to test the stream, label it loudly as a test before you do it.

When the wheel stops, read the winner's name or username exactly as it appears, and keep the result on screen while you screenshot it. Ask the winner to identify themselves in chat if they are watching — a lovely moment when they are — but never make being present a condition unless your rules said so from the start. Changing the rules mid-stream because the winner is absent is the fastest way to turn a celebration into an argument. If your rules do require presence, say the countdown out loud and redraw on camera when it expires.

After the live: close the loop publicly

Within the hour, post the result where the giveaway lived: a comment pinned to the post, a story, a community tab update — with the screenshot of the winning wheel and a tag for the winner. Then contact the winner directly by message to arrange the prize, keeping screenshots of that exchange for your records. Save the stream replay if the platform allows; it is your permanent evidence that the draw was clean, and linking it in the results post settles any late questions before they start. Finally, thank the entrants and say when the next one is coming. A well-run live draw is not just a fair way to end a giveaway — it is the best advertisement for the next one, because everyone who watched knows the prize really gets given away.

A last word on scale: with thousands of entries, the wheel's slices get thin and names unreadable on stream. The honest fix is a two-stage draw — use a random method to shortlist twenty finalists in the open (announce that this is what you are doing), then put those twenty on the wheel for the live finale. The audience still watches the prize get won on the wheel, and the wheel stays readable. Transparency about the process matters more than squeezing every name onto one wheel.

Decision Wheels: Let Chance Decide (Without Regrets)

When every option is fine, the best decision is a fast one — and your reaction to the result is data, too.

There is a particular modern paralysis that has nothing to do with difficult choices. Dinner, a film, a weekend plan, which task to start first — the options are all acceptable, the stakes are tiny, and yet the deciding eats twenty minutes and a measurable amount of goodwill. Decision wheels exist for exactly this swamp. Load the options onto the wheel, spin, done. But used thoughtfully, a decision wheel does something more interesting than picking at random: it reveals what you actually wanted. This guide covers both uses — the wheel as tiebreaker, and the wheel as a mirror.

The real cost of small indecision

Every undecided question taxes the people stuck in it. Groups loop ("I don't mind — what do you want?"), individuals burn willpower they need elsewhere, and the evening quietly shrinks around the choosing instead of the doing. Decision researchers have a blunt way of putting it: for choices where the options are roughly equal, the value of a good decision is small and the value of a fast decision is large. A wheel is a fast-decision machine. It converts "we've been debating restaurants for half an hour" into five seconds of suspense and a plan. The meal was never going to be life-changing; the half hour was real.

Building a good decision wheel

The quality of a decision wheel is set before the spin, by the list. Three rules make lists that produce results people happily accept. First, only include options you would genuinely accept — one joke entry ("gas station sushi") poisons the wheel, because now nobody trusts the outcome and every spin becomes a negotiation. Second, keep options at the same level: "pizza" versus "the Italian place on Fifth Street" versus "cooking" is a muddled wheel; pick a level — cuisines, specific places, or eat-in versus takeaway — and spin one question at a time. Third, cap the list around eight to twelve options. More than that and you have not simplified the decision, you have just decorated it. If the list is longer, spin in two rounds: first to pick a category, then to pick within it.

Weighting is allowed and sometimes wise. If two people mildly prefer Thai and one strongly wants burgers, put Thai on the wheel twice. Duplicated entries take up duplicated slices — the wheel makes the compromise visible and proportional instead of argued. Just agree the weights before spinning, not after the result lands.

The mirror trick: your reaction is the answer

Here is the use nobody expects. Spin the wheel, watch where it lands — and before you obey it, notice how you feel. Relief? Disappointment? A sudden urge to argue that the wheel is wrong? That reaction is information your conscious deliberation failed to surface. If the wheel says "the quiet night in" and your heart sinks, you did not actually want the quiet night in; you wanted permission to want the other thing. Decision coaches have used the coin-flip version of this trick for decades: you do not flip the coin to obey it, you flip it to hear yourself react. The honest rule is: if the result pleases you or leaves you neutral, take it and go. If it genuinely dismays you, you have learned your preference — take the other option, and thank the wheel for the clarity. What you must not do is re-spin until the wheel agrees with you while pretending chance decided; that is the worst of both worlds, slow and dishonest.

When not to use a wheel

Chance is a tool for equal options and low stakes. It is the wrong tool when the options differ in ways that matter and can be known — cost, safety, someone else's strong needs, a deadline only one option meets. It is also the wrong tool for decisions that belong to a person: a wheel should never overrule the birthday child's cake flavour or a colleague's accessibility needs. And it should never be a way to dodge accountability for a real choice at work; "the wheel decided" is charming for lunch and unacceptable for a budget. The line is simple: if you would be genuinely content with every slice, spin. If you would not, the decision deserves thought, not confetti.

Making it a habit that sticks

Groups that adopt a wheel for small decisions report a side benefit: deciding gets funnier and lighter, because the wheel absorbs the blame. Keep a few standing lists — Friday lunch places, film genres, weekend activities, who makes tea — and the spin becomes a ritual rather than a debate. The same tool that runs a fair giveaway or picks a student in a classroom turns out to be just as good at rescuing Friday night. Spin it, accept it, and spend the saved twenty minutes on the thing you chose.

Frequently Asked Questions

Straight answers about the wheel, the odds and your entries.

Is the wheel really random?

Yes. Each spin's final resting angle is chosen by your browser's built-in random number generator before the animation plays, and every entry occupies an equal slice of the wheel. No entry is weighted, boosted or remembered between spins, so each spin is independent of the last — a name that just won has exactly the same chance on the next spin as everyone else, unless you remove it.

How many entries can the wheel hold?

There is no hard limit, and the wheel will happily draw a few hundred slices. In practice, labels become hard to read beyond roughly one hundred entries on a normal screen. For very large draws, spin in rounds or shortlist finalists first — the live winner guide explains a fair two-stage approach for big audiences.

Can an entry have a bigger chance than the others?

Yes — simply enter it more than once. Each line becomes its own slice, so a name listed twice has double the chance of a name listed once. It is a transparent way to weight a raffle (for example, giving repeat customers an extra entry) because the audience can see the extra slice on the wheel.

What happens when someone wins — can I draw again?

After each spin you can spin again with the same list, or remove the winner and spin for the next prize. Removing winners is the right approach for multi-prize giveaways, since it guarantees nobody can take two prizes. The full routine is in our fair giveaway guide.

Are my entries saved or sent anywhere?

No. Your list is processed entirely in your browser on your own device. SpinTheWheelHub has no accounts and no entry database; nothing you type into the wheel is transmitted to or stored on a server. If you refresh the page, the sample list returns — so for an important draw, keep your master list in your own document and paste it in when needed.

Does shuffling change the odds?

No. Every slice is the same size wherever it sits, so reordering the list cannot change anyone's probability. Shuffling is still worth doing before a public draw: it looks fairer, mixes neighbours apart, and stops anyone claiming the list was arranged. Fairness you can see is worth almost as much as fairness itself.

Can I use the wheel for a real money prize or a regulated draw?

The wheel is designed for giveaways, classrooms, games and everyday decisions. It is not a certified gambling or lottery device, and promotions involving significant money or legal regulation may require official procedures and permits depending on where you live. For those, follow the rules that apply to your draw; for everything else, spin away.

The wheel landed between two names. Who won?

That cannot actually happen. The pointer always settles on exactly one slice, and the winner announced under the wheel is calculated from the wheel's final angle — not judged by eye. Trust the announced name; it is the mathematically correct slice for where the wheel stopped.

Can I use SpinTheWheelHub in my classroom or stream?

Absolutely — that is what it is for. Teachers can find ready-made ideas in the classroom guide, and streamers can find the full live-draw routine in the Instagram and YouTube Live guide. No permission or credit is required for ordinary use of the tool.
